What I want to do in this 3rd one is try to simplify this for you to help you understand why we don't rank keywords with anchors, and from that, why we also don't rank pages with links in the broader sense.

A small note, that linking DIRECTLY to a page, does give it a bit more of a boost than than linking to other pages, but, you cannot over-do this otherwise the links are seen as unnatural. In general, it's best to just forget about trying to rank pages with links, and focus instead on "increasing site authority with links as naturally as possible".

Now, here's the PRECISE and EXACT reason why you don't rank KEYWORDS with anchor texts.

In my business I get so so many clients.. My god.. I think I've answered this 3 or 4 times TODAY alone. No joke. The last one was just 5 minutes ago, and I've not even hit send yet.

One Line Answer

Google no longer has a concept of "keywords". They got rid of this a few years ago.

Longer Answer

Yes, I hear a bunch of you in the back there saying "WHAT! Are you mad??"

But, it's true..

Why do you think "keyword density" is no longer a thing...

Why do you think Google stopped showing keywords people search for in the search console. (I don't know the exact mentality behind it. Probably multiple things, one of them being they don't want people to focus on keywords anymore, and think more about writing for the user, instead of writing to capture keywords)

This is why years ago, if you searched for "top toasters", you'd get pages with "Top Toasters" in the title, and lots of anchor texts with "top toasters" ranking.

And if you searched for "best toasters", you'd get "Best Toasters" pages.

And if you searched for "toaster reviews", you'd get pages targeting "Toaster Reviews" in the title, h1, h2, anchors etc.

See [https://www.blackhatworld.com/seo/g...e-engineered-the-core-updates-part-1.1514379/](https://www.blackhatworld.com/seo/guide-how-google-works-reverse-engineered-the-core-updates-part-1.1514379/) \- "Hummingbird Update - 2013"

I won't go too heavy into details here, you can read a little more on my "Google updates history" guide..

But Hummingbird was the start. It was no longer just keywords match blindly to pages based on weighted rules like keywords in title, h1, h2, kw density and anchor texts.

It's changed year on year, and with various updates to where we are at today, which is now :-

There is no longer any concept of "keywords". People are not searching for "keywords" and being matched with pages in an old school search and match way.

We have "search queries".

Every search query has a "user intention" behind it.

Google then matches user intents with pages.

So.. Past

Keyword1 -> match(strength: 95/100) -> page\_X

Keyword2 -> match(strength: 83/100) -> page\_X

Keyword3 -> match(strength: 78/100) -> page\_X

Keyword1 -> match(strength: 98/100) -> page\_Y

Keyword2 -> match(strength: 89/100) -> page\_Y

Keyword3 -> match(strength: 71/100) -> page\_Y

Y beats X for keyword 1, assuming PageRank is equal.

That was really it. That was the old algo simplified.

If X had more PR than Y, then X would probably win, but if X's relevancy for Keyword1 was say, 70, then it wouldn't matter how strong it was, it would never beat Y. This is why, you could beat authority sites in the past by just creating pages targeting keywords where they had no relevance.

Ie, authority site A targets 'best toasters', but not "top toasters", you can then target "top toasters" and outrank them with no backlinks, or very few backlinks. This is why SEO was so easy, and it was keyword based.

Today

Keywords are now "search queries". We could call them "keywords" if you want, but, a keyword implies it's a simple "keyword search". "search query", implies there's something more complicated going on. A translation inbetween, and we aren't searching for the "search query" the way we search for a "keyword".

We'll call them SK's

We'll call "user intent" UI for short.

SK1 -> match(strength: 93/100) -> UI\_X

SK2 -> match(strength 83/100) -> UI\_X

SK3 -> match(strength 75/100) -> UI\_X

SK1 -> match(strength: 93/100) -> UI\_Y

SK2 -> match(strength 83/100) -> UI\_Y

SK3 -> match(strength 75/100) -> UI\_Y

Now, you see we have search queries matching to user intents?

And they match to MULTIPLE user intents.

It's so hard to just write small articles. There's just so much to teach in SEO.. :-/ So many sub-lessons that have to be taught in order to understand the upper level concept.

Many people talk about only 1 user intent for search queries, but it's more complex than this.

There are multiple people, each with multiple intents.

So one person can have more than one intent.

I like to think of each person having a primary intent, and a few secondary intents.

If you search for "buy backlinks"

The primary intent in this case of almost all of the people is to buy backlinks for their website in order to rank higher on Google. (Some might want backlinks to rank higher on bing lol)

But, their secondary intents might be things like

"What kind of backlinks can I buy"

"How much do backlinks cost"

"what are good providers of backlinks"

So these are all DIRECT things that can be included in pages that Google might rank for this user intent.

You could also go deeper here and have something called "tertiary intents".

These are intents I think of that you wouldn't include directly in the article, but you would LINK to and cover in other articles. This can help build a topical graph around your article.

For this one we might have

"Do backlinks work for ranking on Google"

"Where can I find free backlinks?"

"What kind of anchors should I use for my backlinks?"

"What types of backlinks can you get?"

Do you see?

The secondary intents are specifically related to the main intent. It's like sub-questions, or sub-thoughts that may or may not be conscious in the person's mind. They may not be thinking "What kind of backlinks can I buy", but, if you show this to them, if their primary intent is to "buy backlinks", then they are probably going to be interested in this. They're likewise going to be interested in learning about good providers where they can buy, or prices etc.

The tertiary, they won't necessarily be wanting to read NOW about "do backlinks work", or "where can I find free backlinks", or "what types can I get", but it's topics that some people looking to buy could be interested in reading later, or reading after they've read and satsified the main intent.. That's why they should be internal links to other articles.

Ok so back to this bit

We'll call them SK's

We'll call "user intent" UI for short.

SK1 -> match(strength: 93/100) -> UI\_X

SK2 -> match(strength 83/100) -> UI\_X

SK3 -> match(strength 75/100) -> UI\_X

SK1 -> match(strength: 93/100) -> UI\_Y

SK2 -> match(strength 83/100) -> UI\_Y

SK3 -> match(strength 75/100) -> UI\_Y

Now you understand what UI\_X and UI\_Y is.

They could also be 2 primary user intents, or they could be a primary and secondary. They'll have scores for tertiary too, although they won't "call" them primary/secondary/tertiary. This is just something for us, the SEO's/writers/creators to differentiate and structure. Ie, primary intent is the main focus(title/h1/initial paragraphs) and should be dealt with quickly and secondary are the things useful that you also cover after the primary in the article, then the tertiary are the related articles you internally link to.

So when you do "keyword research"(I still call it this), really what you're doing is search query to UI\_XXX research. You want to group keywords/search queries and write user intents for them, then label and match those UI's to each search keyword and label it as a primary, secondary or teritary for that search query. Then from that you can craft article articles that match search queries/user intents properly.

You might even have a secondary intent like "How much do backlinks cost" which you label as UI\_31

It can be a secondary UI for search keyword "buy backlinks", and a primary for keyword "backlinks cost".

As the secondary, you would have a paragraph or 2 about it, and if you do a separate article as a primary, you link to that, and have an entire in-depth article on it.

Likewise, here, we have a secondary intent "How do I do keyword research with this new idea of search queries and user intents".

The primary intent of this article is "Why don't you rank keywords with anchors?" - If you clicked this, you're probably asking that question, right? Or at least something similar.

But to simplify it, and to try to end this long guide.

All you want to focus on with your anchors is just creating context between the article with the link, and your page.

so if we're getting a link to our new "search intent guide".

Yeah, we might have an exact like "search intent" in this case, because this particular type of article isn't really focused on just a small number of important search queries/keywords, like a "best toaster" one would be.

That's why in the case of the best toaster article, having lots of "best toaster" anchors is more dangerous. For broader, bigger articles that will rank for a lot of different search queries this is less of an issue, but you still follow these rules :-

1) Try not to use "keyword anchors".

1A) If you use them, aim not to repeat them, at least don't repeat them often.

2) Try to be as varied as you can with your anchors, and think about phrases that are contextually related to your page that would appear in an article.

Examples:-

"search intent is the most important concept"

"user intent matters more now"

"learn more in this guide to search intent"

"their guide to search intent"

"they explain search intent"

"search intent finally clicked"

"this helped me understand user intent"

"backlinko's guide to search intent"

You see?

It's contextually relevant. It has MEANING.

These are all good points and I've also highlighted that about keywords in my on-page guide.

But.. This guide is a bit different, and it's not so much about on-page or how to write the content exactly.

It's more about how Google switched from the concept of matching keywords. Ie, the TYPE of search they do. It's like a fundamental change in how the search engine works.

They're no longer just searching for keywords in pages, but translating the keyword into a user intent, and then looking to see which pages "answer", or "satisfy" that user intent.

I personally, don't even think about keywords anymore when doing content.

In fact, I'm working on an AI content SaaS and in doing so, this forces me to understand this stuff at a deep level, since my AI has to write content. I don't have it focus at all on keywords. Instead it revolves around user intents. Primary, secondary and tertiary(as I discuss in detail about). That's exactly where I got this concept from that I write about above. All that info above is coming from my experience over the past 2 years with AI content writers and building my own to write content in as close as possible to the perfect way to rank.

Even in my example above, you can see the uselessness of keywords..

The page I was analysing - [https://searchengineland.com/search-intent-more-types-430814](https://searchengineland.com/search-intent-more-types-430814)

(As I was writing the guide, I was acting as if I would be writing this as an SEO article on my site, and giving examples from that perspective how I would build topical authority around it, and what kind of things I'd cover)

This page is #14 for "intent mapping".

Yet, you won't find even the keyword "map", let alone "mapping" or "intent mapping" anywhere on the page.

This would be \*impossible\* in 2012. The only way it would rank for that in 2012 without the keyword on the page, would be if it had a few anchors with "intent mapping".

There's really no clear benefit in my experience to writing a keyword multiple times, adding it into more H2's and of course using it in anchors.

Obviously, if you want to rank a page for "Best Toasters", you have to say "best toasters" a few times. If you write about salmon, curtains and tables, you aren't going to rank for best toasters.

The point isn't that you don't need "keywords" in your article, but it's that you don't need to THINK about keywords anymore.

If you want to write a best toaster guides, you don't need to list out keywords and try to use them. You are of course going to write "best toasters" in the title/h1. You'll probably use it in a 1 or 2 h2's.

The only time you maybe focus more on actual keywords is for more obscure keywords/search queries. Like you might be trying to rank for some specific model of something like "JEHDU873", and in that case, you would make sure you mention it a few times.

But even then, you still want to be focused around the user intent. If the search query is "JEHDU873" and that's a model of a TV, then the search intent might be "Get information on the TV JEHDU873", so just mentioning that keyword with trash info might not rank you (Unless there's no pages targetting it in which case you can rank with just keywords and gibberish)

So in summary here, what we want to do is not necessarily stop talking about keywords, but understand that instead of having

keyword -> use keywords on content

We have intermediate translatory steps

keyword -> user intent -> satsify user intent on content

Ie, this is what I do with my AI writer. She doesn't write with the idea of keywords. She writes with the idea of "what does the user want to learn here"

so if a keyword is "best toasters", then the primary intent is "Learn about the best toasters I can buy"

secondary intents will be like

"What kind of prices are toasters"

"What features do toasters have"

"Is there a difference between modern toasters, or are they all similar"

"Are there different types of toasters"

"What are the benefits of type X vs type Y"

A tertiary intent might be

"How can I make the most delicious cheese toastie in the world" ![:)](<Base64-Image-Removed>)

so you see, that from a keyword, we get intents. We don't care about big lists of keywords. Google will naturally match all those keywords to the INTENT we are already targeting. By targeting the intent, we are naturally targeting the right keywords.

It's now a translation. It's no longer keywords matched on page. It's keywords grouped by intents, and intents matched to pages.

And of course, by satsifying these, you are going to be using "keywords" on the page, but that's not how Google matches you. They are using machine learning algorithms to identify if a page satsifies an intent.

A big change too, that I didn't mention in the main article.

All the "helpful content updates" are actually "engagement updates".

It's still hard for Google to determine if a page satsifies user intent. Their algorithms were determining that shitty AI content was doing this.

AI \*CAN\* determine this, but you need big sophisticated LLMs, and they don't use those. They are WAY too resource intensive.

Google's helpful content update, was in fact them just giving more strength to engagement signals. Chrome signals, users hitting back and then looking at what they do next etc. Note: This is very different than clicking. Clicking a link in a serp isn't an engagement metric.

If someone clicks, hits back 10 seconds later and then searches for something new. They got their answer.

If someone clicks, hits back 10 seconds later, and clicks another link, they didn't.

This is why satsifying user intent properly is so important in 2024. More important than 2022. Users are grading pages.

People might notice that sometimes new sites will start to rank for a lot of keywords, then drop. Especially low quality AI content. This is because of this effect. Google has tested it with engagement and then it drops because the engagement was low.

A final point about the AI..

They won't use low param LLMs.

LLMs are specifically models for generalizing. The name is deceiving. They aren't meant to be models to understand language exactly. They are models to perform reasoning, through an understanding of language that lets them learn. Ie, you learn to read, then you can study.

BERT etc on the other hand completely understands language. It just doesn't have the ability to go a step further and use that understanding to learn to reason.

But BERT and these other transformer models can be trained to do various language tasks like determine if a chunk of text answer a question, determine what the intent of sentence(keyword/search query) is, determine

There's so much you can do with these transformer models.. Like this here [https://github.com/MaartenGr/KeyBERT](https://github.com/MaartenGr/KeyBERT)

You can create document level embeddings, paragraph level embeddings and sentence level embeddings and then do various things with each.

That KeyBERT for example is really simple and has nothing to do with user intent, but it shows how BERT understands language, and can already extract "keyword".

Notice one it extracts in the sample is "learning function", which isn't even a keyword used on the page.

BERT btw isn't a seq2seq model, which means it doesn't generate output.

It has 2 modes, one is masked language modelling, where it predicts a missing word, and the other next sentence prediction, where it predicts if the next sentence follows.

It can only do things like classifications on binary or multi-labels, or finding the position in a text.

It could be used to determine TYPES of intents, like buying, information, researching, learning. They could have many types.

How they PHYSICALLY determine user intent.. I haven't thought of as it's not that important, but..

They're probably not directly using AI for this in the sense that they feed a query and get an answer. It's likely something they determine through a combination of multiple machine learning models, and data feedback from searches.

Ie, when someone searches for "jaguar", even a human can't necessarly say what the intent is. There are many ranging from information on the car, the cat. They'll learn based on what people search for next. If everyone searched for 'jaguar', was shown cars, then immediately searched for 'jaguar cat', then Google would 100% start showing only cats for 'jaguar'.

For us, we leverage bigger LLMs to understand intent, AND, we or our AIs can search Google and see what ranks. THIS tells us the intent. Google themselves mostly tell us what the intent is based on what they rank.

Just want to add an argument on this. Not to debate, but it is something I like to share.

There are many ways to use AI, or LLMs, to determine/analyze pages while being accurate and resource-efficient.

Per example:

1\. Use 2b-3b to filter out 100% trash pages

2\. Use 7b-ish to analyze medium-quality pages

3\. Use 10b+ to rerank top 100 pages, which is all that matters

Or.

1\. Use 2b-3b for every page low authority websites publish

2\. Use 10b+ for every page the top dogs publish

Or.

1\. Use whatever T5, bert, or whatever cheap-to-run model as you said to do the first filter

2\. Then use accurate 2b, 7b to dig deeper for pages that pass the first filter

You don't have to use expensive LLM for every page, you can have a dynamic LLM tier setup.

Also, the 8Bs are incredibly, insanely powerful right now. Gemini flash is insanely good. Claude's Haiku 3.5 must be good too, which I haven't test yet.

I believe the 2b, 3bs are good enough to be the first filter, which is very cheap to run.

And Google can afford to use the 8b or Gemini-pro level LLM to analyze pages that matter the most, maybe only 0.001% of the total pages.
